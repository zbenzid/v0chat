## 1. NLP / PNL: what it claims, what the literature says, what to keep and what to drop

### Takeaway
NLP as a system is not supported: every systematic review found (Sharpley 1984/1987, Heap 1988, Witkowski 2010, Sturt et al. 2012, Passmore & Rowson 2019) concludes that its distinctive claims (preferred representational systems / VAK matching, eye-accessing cues, the specific "Milton model" machinery) lack empirical backing. Several language habits that marketers label "PNL" do work, but for reasons established by mainstream psycholinguistics and consumer research (presupposition, self-referencing "you", concreteness, sensory words, if-then planning), so the script system should keep the technique and drop the NLP theory and vocabulary.

### Cited Findings

**What NLP claims (as used in copywriting)**
- Practitioner sources describe the Milton Model as NLP's codification of Milton Erickson's hypnotic language: deliberately "artfully vague" wording, presuppositions, embedded commands, linguistic binds, reframing and "future pacing" (projecting the reader into a desired future) — [abccopywriting.com](https://www.abccopywriting.com/?p=1965); [O'Reilly, Web Copy That Sells, "Hypnotically persuasive language"](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/web-copy-that/9780814432518/xhtml/ch5sec4.html); [AWAI, "Using assumptions and presuppositions in your copy"](https://awai.com/2009/09/using-assumptions-and-presuppostions-in-your-copy)
- NLP's own lexicons define embedded commands as calls to action hidden inside a longer sentence, attributed to Erickson and modelled by Bandler and Grinder; the same source flags the technique as potentially manipulative — [Landsiedel NLP lexicon](https://www.landsiedel.com/en/nlp/nlp-lexikon/eingebettete-befehle.php)
- "Copywriting PNL" is sold in French as a ready-made skill set (presuppositions, embedded commands, future pacing, sensory predicates) — practitioner opinion only, no data — [findskill.ai, Copywriting PNL](https://findskill.ai/fr/skills/ai-creative/copywriting-pnl/)
- The only academic-style work found on Milton-model patterns in advertising is descriptive (a stylistic analysis of KFC ads); it counts the patterns but does not test whether audiences respond differently — [Iraqi Academic Scientific Journals, 2025](https://iasj.rdd.edu.iq/journals/uploads/2025/12/28/bd24a0cb110fb4bd126a503a4313d8cf.pdf)

**What reviews and tests say**
- Witkowski (2010, Polish Psychological Bulletin 41(2), 58–66) reviewed the "NLP Research Data Base" maintained by NLP proponents (315 articles), kept 63 published in ISI-listed journals, and found only 18.2% of the relevant studies supportive of NLP's tenets; he concludes the results contradict the claim that NLP has an empirical basis and calls it pseudoscience — [DOI 10.2478/v10059-010-0008-0](https://doi.org/10.2478/v10059-010-0008-0); summary at [Donald Clark, Plan B](https://donaldclarkplanb.blogspot.com/2010/11/nlp-scientific-paper-suggests-its.html)
- Sturt et al. (2012, British Journal of General Practice 62(604), e757–e764) screened 1,459 titles and included 10 experimental studies (5 RCTs, 5 pre-post). Four of the five RCTs found no significant between-group difference; risk of bias was "high or uncertain" across all studies. Conclusion: "There is little evidence that NLP interventions improve health-related outcomes. This conclusion reflects the limited quantity and quality of NLP research, rather than robust evidence of no effect." — [PMC3481516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3481516)
- A Freedom of Information request cited by King's College London found the NHS spent over £800,000 on NLP in 2006–2009 despite this evidence gap — [King's College London](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/archive/news/nmpc/2012/little-evidence-for-nlp-in-healthcare)
- Wiseman et al. (2012, PLoS ONE 7(7): e40259) ran three studies on the NLP claim that looking up-right signals lying and up-left signals truth: eye movements of liars and truth-tellers did not match the NLP pattern (Study 1), training people in the NLP rule did not improve lie detection (Study 2), and coded press-conference footage showed no difference (Study 3). "Taken together the results of the three studies fail to support the claims of NLP." — [PMC3394779](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3394779)
- Sharpley's 1984 review (15 studies) and 1987 follow-up (7 more) found no reproducible support for "preferred representational systems" or for matching a listener's sensory predicates; Heap (1988) reached the same verdict on representational systems — [Heap, ASKE skeptics](https://aske-skeptics.org.uk/nlp4.html); pro-NLP rebuttal (argues predicate matching is only one of several NLP techniques, an advocate's view, not peer reviewed) at [Inspiritive](https://www.inspiritive.com.au/nlp-research-reviews/)
- Passmore & Rowson (2019, International Coaching Psychology Review 14(1), 57–69) conclude that unique NLP practices are poorly supported by research evidence — [University of Reading CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/91275)
- VAK-style "matching" has the same problem in education: Pashler et al. (2008) found very few studies with a design able to test the "meshing hypothesis", and several of those "flatly contradict" it — [Pashler et al., Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/)
- Hidden or subliminal influence, the idea behind "embedded commands", has very weak effects: Trappey's (1996) meta-analysis of 23 studies of subliminal advertising found "very little effect" on consumer choice — [Trappey 1996](https://ir.lib.nycu.edu.tw/bitstream/11536/14246/1/A1996UZ40600005.pdf)

**Mainstream findings that overlap with "NLP" techniques**
- Presupposition: in Loftus & Zanni (1975), asking "Did you see *the* broken headlight?" rather than "*a* broken headlight" made significantly more viewers report a headlight that was not there; the definite article presupposes existence, and a false presupposition can be stored as new information — [Loftus 1975 paper](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf); [UCSD course notes on presupposition](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/Courses/101c/presupposition.pdf)
- "You" language: Cruz, Leonhardt & Pezzuti (2017, Journal of Interactive Marketing 39, 104–116) report that second-person pronouns in brand posts raise consumer involvement and brand attitude through self-referencing — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v39y2017icp104-116.html)
- Concreteness: Packard & Berger (2021, Journal of Consumer Research 47(5), 787–806), five studies including more than 1,000 real customer-service interactions: concrete wording raised satisfaction, purchase intent and actual purchases, because it signals the speaker is listening — [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/how-language-boosts-customer-satisfaction/)
- Sensory words (the valid core of "VAK predicates"): Cascio Rizzo, Berger, De Angelis & Pozharliev (2023, Journal of Consumer Research 50(4), 810–825) analysed Instagram and TikTok posts plus experiments: sensory language ("juicy", "crumble" versus "good") increases likes, comments and willingness to buy, because viewers infer the influencer really used the product (perceived authenticity). No matching to a viewer's "system" is involved — [Wharton working paper](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Sesory-Language-and-Influencers.pdf)
- If-then planning (the valid core of "future pacing"): Gollwitzer & Sheeran's (2006) meta-analysis of 94 tests found implementation intentions improve goal attainment with d = 0.65 — [University of Konstanz repository](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/eb48c8cf-7855-478e-90ec-1ea6d44f8252/content). A 2024 update on 642 tests reportedly gives much lower bias-corrected estimates (d = 0.35 with trim-and-fill, d = 0.15 with robust Bayesian meta-analysis); this figure comes from a secondary source — [The Behavioral Scientist glossary](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/glossary/implementation-intentions)
- "Vous êtes libre de…" (a French-origin compliance technique often folded into "PNL" training): Carpenter's 2013 meta-analysis found it effective — [Carpenter 2013](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/24608) — but a 2023 pre-registered meta-analysis of 52 experiments (N = 19,528) found g = 0.44 overall and no effect in low-risk-of-bias studies (g = 0.11, 95% CI −0.18 to 0.40) — [Fillon et al., Meta-Psychology](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2640)

- The honest framing for the user: "la PNL" is a brand name wrapped around a few sound copywriting habits and a lot of unsupported theory. A script framework can cite the underlying research and never mention NLP.
- The persuasion risk of NLP-style copy is ethical as much as scientific: presuppositions and "embedded" formulas are tools for getting a claim past the viewer without stating it, which is exactly what consumer law treats as misleading when the claim is false (section 8).

## 2. Attention and curiosity: what makes people keep watching

### Takeaway
The best-supported levers for holding attention are easy-to-process, concrete language, emotions that carry uncertainty (anxiety, excitement, hope), story structure and a strong peak and ending. The popular "Zeigarnik effect / open loop" justification is weaker than believed: a 2025 meta-analysis finds no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks, though the urge to resume something interrupted (Ovsiankina effect) is real.

### Cited Findings
- Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina: Ghibellini & Meier's 2025 meta-analysis (Humanities & Social Sciences Communications) concludes "the Ovsiankina effect represents a general tendency, whereas the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity"; the memory advantage for unfinished tasks "has proven particularly difficult to replicate" — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [University of Bern PDF](https://www.kog.psy.unibe.ch/e48276/e1785865/s41599-025-05000-w1_ger.pdf)
- What holds attention in text: Berger, Moe & Schweidel (2023, Journal of Marketing) analysed over 600,000 reading sessions across 35,000 pieces of content plus experiments. Anxious, exciting and hopeful language kept people reading; sad language made them leave. The driver is arousal plus uncertainty: emotions that make people feel certain reduce sustained attention. Language that is easier to process, including concrete wording ("tomorrow" rather than "soon"), encourages continued reading — [American Marketing Association summary](https://www.ama.org/2023/03/21/the-challenge-of-keeping-an-audience-engaged-how-language-shapes-attention/)
- Simplicity: Shulman, Markowitz & Rogers (2024, Science Advances) used Washington Post headline tests on nearly 20,000 stories: simpler headlines (common words, shorter, more readable) got more clicks and were better remembered; the effect is small per item; journalists themselves showed no preference for simple headlines — [Harvard Kennedy School](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/media/readers-prefer-simpler-headlines-unless-they-are-journalists); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-06-simple-headlines-online-news-readers.html)
- Negativity: Robertson et al. (2023, Nature Human Behaviour, Registered Report) analysed about 105,000 Upworthy headline variants from A/B tests (5.7 million clicks, 370 million impressions): negative words raised click-through, positive words lowered it; sadness words drove clicks, anger words did not — [PMC10202797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
- Headline language at scale: Banerjee & Urminsky (2025, Marketing Science 44(3), 566–592) use thousands of Upworthy field experiments to test cognitive, motivational, affective and grammatical drivers of engagement — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mksc.2021.0018); [working paper](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf). Their specific results on curiosity and concreteness could not be retrieved.
- Story: van Laer et al. (2014, Journal of Consumer Research 40(5), 797–817) meta-analysed 132 effect sizes from 76 articles on narrative transportation. Factors the writer controls reliably increase transportation: identifiable characters, a plot that evokes mental imagery, and events that seem plausible — [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [OnFiction summary](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html?m=0)
- Peak-end: Alaybek et al. (2022, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170) meta-analysed 174 effect sizes: the peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations is large (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487–0.661), and duration has almost no effect — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)
- Faces: Bakhshi, Shamma & Gilbert (2014, CHI) on about 1.1 million Instagram photos: photos with faces were 38% more likely to get likes and 32% more likely to get comments, controlling for reach and activity — [Georgia Tech](https://www.news.gatech.edu/news/2014/03/20/face-it-instagram-pictures-faces-are-more-popular)
- First seconds in video ads: a University of Twente MSc eye-tracking thesis on TikTok ads reports a three-second engagement threshold, text elements as first fixation point more often than faces, and longer viewing for native-looking than high-production visuals. Student thesis, preliminary — [University of Twente](https://essay.utwente.nl/fileshare/file/109005/Daphne_Giuzio_MSc_Thesis.pdf)

- For video, "certainty" and "uncertainty" pull in different directions: uncertainty holds attention (Berger et al. 2023) while confident wording earns likes and shares (Pezzuti et al. 2021, section 6). A workable reading is: open with an unresolved question, deliver the answer in confident, concrete terms.
- The text-based findings above come from articles and headlines. Applying them to spoken Reels scripts is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Loewenstein (1994, Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75–98) proposed the information-gap theory of curiosity: curiosity arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know, and it peaks when people know something but not everything. Kang et al. (2009, Psychological Science) found curiosity highest at intermediate confidence and linked to better memory for answers. Neither was re-sourced; the search budget ran out.
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) The orienting response to cuts, motion and novelty (Annie Lang's limited-capacity model), the von Restorff isolation effect, and cognitive load theory are established in laboratory research, but I found no session source and no study testing them on Reels or TikTok retention.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests "open loops" or "pattern interrupts" as scripted devices in short-form video.
- The specific Banerjee & Urminsky results could not be extracted.
- The claim that "71% of viewers decide in the first 3 seconds" circulates in blogs with no source — [example, tlinky.com](https://tlinky.com/3-second-hook/). Treat as unsupported.

## 3. Sharing and virality: what makes people send, share and tag

### Takeaway
Sharing is driven by high-arousal emotion (awe, anger, anxiety, amusement) rather than by sadness or mere positivity, by usefulness, and by what the share says about the sender and does for their relationships. Moral-emotional language reliably increases diffusion but the effect is smaller than first reported (about 12–15% per word, not 20%) and is mostly measured on political content.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman (2012, Journal of Marketing Research 49, 192–205), about 7,000 New York Times articles plus experiments: content evoking high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger, anxiety) emotion is more viral; low-arousal emotion (sadness) is less viral; results hold when controlling for how surprising, interesting or practically useful the content is; positive content is more viral than negative overall — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-secret-to-online-success-what-makes-content-go-viral/)
- Moral-emotional language: Brady et al. (2017, PNAS) found each added moral-emotional word raised retweets by about 20% across roughly 563,000 political tweets — [NYU CSMaP](https://csmapnyu.org/research/academic-research/emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks). Burton, Cruz & Hahn (2021, Nature Human Behaviour) showed the model predicted no better than an implausible "XYZ contagion" model — [Copenhagen Business School](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/reconsidering-evidence-of-moral-contagion-in-online-social-networ/). A 2025 pre-registered replication (N = 849,266) found IRR = 1.15, and a meta-analysis of 27 studies found 12% more sharing per moral-emotional word — [Kellogg School](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2025/estimating-the-effect-size-of-moral-contagion-in-online/)
- Controversy: Chen & Berger (2013, Journal of Consumer Research 40(3), 580–593): controversy increases the likelihood of discussion at low levels, but beyond a moderate level more controversy decreases it. Controversy raises interest and also discomfort; when people's identity is disclosed, controversy fails to boost conversation, and when they are anonymous, moderate controversy increases it — [University of Pennsylvania repository](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/39527); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/finding-companys-controversy-sweet-spot/)
- Sharing motives: the New York Times Customer Insight Group's "Psychology of Sharing" (2011; qualitative phases plus a survey of more than 2,500 medium-to-heavy sharers) identified five motivations, all centred on relationships: bringing valuable and entertaining content to others, defining ourselves to others, growing and nourishing relationships, self-fulfilment, and spreading the word about causes or brands — [Business Wire release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20110713005971/en/New-York-Times-Completes-Research-%E2%80%98Psychology-Sharing%E2%80%99). Industry survey, self-reported, not peer reviewed.
- Out-group negativity: in the Robertson et al. Upworthy registered report, negativity about out-groups raised engagement by 14.1% versus 1.6% for negativity about in-groups (as reported by a secondary summary) — [Harvard Chan health communication](https://www.hsph.harvard.edu/chc/2023/05/01/negative-news-headline-more-clicks/)
- Platform side: Instagram's head Adam Mosseri is reported to have named watch time, likes per reach and sends per reach as the three most important ranking signals (January 2025), with sends mattering more for reaching non-followers — [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources//instagram-algorithms/); [Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/instagram-sends-per-reach-playbook/). Secondary coverage; the original statement was not retrieved.
- TikTok data: a 2026 University of Chicago thesis found emotional cues have "low but statistically significant explanatory power" for shares and comments; surprise and fear were consistently positive, arousal categories were less informative than discrete, time-specific cues, and part of the link is produced by algorithmic amplification — [UChicago Knowledge](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/rs03s-c0f50)

| Moderate controversy | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (one paper, five studies; sound logic) | "Opinion impopulaire : poster tous les jours ne sert à rien." | The position is defensible and argued; topic is low-stakes for the audience (taste, method), not identity or politics |
| Relatability | PRACTITIONER LORE (overlaps with self-referencing, section 6) | "Toi aussi tu réécris ton hook quinze fois ?" | The situation is specific enough that a non-target viewer would not recognise it |

- Because comments on Reels are public and tied to a profile, Chen & Berger's identity-disclosure finding suggests keeping controversy mild: people avoid discussing divisive topics when their name is attached.
- "Sends" are a private act toward one person, so the relationship motive ("this is for you") is probably a better script target than broadcast sharing. This is an inference; no study of DM-sending motives on Reels was found.

### Gaps
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Berger & Milkman's per-emotion odds (I recall about +34% for anger and +30% for awe per standard deviation, with sadness negative) were not confirmed from the paper this session.
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Jonah Berger's STEPPS framework (Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories) from "Contagious" (2013) is a synthesis for practitioners; its components vary in evidence. No source was retrieved.
- The NYT study's per-motivation percentages differ between secondary sources and could not be verified against the original deck.
- No academic research on "dark social" or DM-sending drivers for short video was found. Vendor statistics on dark social exist but were not checked.
- No study isolates "tag a friend" or "this is so me" prompts.

## 4. Persuasion: getting people to act

### Takeaway
The classic compliance principles are real but small and conditional: foot-in-the-door effects are around r = 0.1–0.17, scarcity cues raise purchase intentions on average with clear moderators, and gain/loss framing replicates internationally with attenuated size, while "loss aversion" as a universal law is contested. For CTAs, the robust rule is to make the action easy and give a real reason.

### Cited Findings
- Foot-in-the-door: meta-analyses find a small effect (overall r between about .09 and .17; a 1984 meta-analysis gave r = .17 for foot-in-the-door and .15 for door-in-the-face "even under optimal conditions"); the effect weakens when the two requests are unrelated — [Penn State, sequential-request meta-analysis](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/sequentialrequest-persuasive-strategies-metaanalysis-of-footinthe/); [Burger 1999 review](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/burger/2497673)
- Scarcity: Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022, Journal of Retailing 98(4), 741–758) meta-analysed 416 effect sizes from 131 studies: scarcity cues generally increase purchase intentions; demand-based scarcity works best for utilitarian products, supply-based for experiences, time-based for high-involvement products — [Monash University](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/scarcity-tactics-in-marketing-a-meta-analysis-of-product-scarcity/)
- Prospect theory framing: Ruggeri et al. (2020, Nature Human Behaviour), over 4,000 participants in 19 countries: the original patterns "broadly replicate, with only minor exceptions and somewhat smaller effect sizes"; 12 of 13 theoretical contrasts replicated — [Vrije Universiteit record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/replicating-patterns-of-prospect-theory-for-decision-under-risk/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/05/200518144913.htm)
- Loss aversion is disputed: Gal & Rucker (2018) argue it has been overgeneralised; Mrkva et al. (2020) reply that it is "alive and well"; a 2022 paper replicated Mrkva's paradigm but found no loss aversion in five studies with symmetrically presented gains and losses (n = 2,001), weak loss aversion at about $40 (mean λ = 1.16) and clearer at $100 (λ = 1.54) — [Undark](https://undark.org/2021/11/15/among-social-scientists-a-vigorous-debate-over-loss-aversion/); [Judgment and Decision Making 2022](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/22/220414/jdm220414.html)
- The "because" effect: Langer, Blank & Chanowitz (1978, JPSP 36(6), 635–642) had an experimenter ask to cut in line at a copier with no reason, a placebic reason ("because I have to make copies") or a real reason, for a small or a large favour — [original paper PDF](https://jamesclear.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/copy-machine-study-ellen-langer.pdf). No replication or meta-analysis was found this session.
- Parasocial relationships: a meta-analysis of 117 studies (47,647 respondents) finds parasocial engagement with influencers relates to brand associations and purchase intentions, with product-dependent effects — [University of Jyväskylä](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_97674); Han & Balabanis (2023) model 250 effect sizes from 58 studies on influencer source credibility — [University of East Anglia](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/101563/1/rba13-Han_Balabanis_Meta-analys.doc). Mostly correlational survey data.
- Rhetorical questions: Petty, Cacioppo & Heesacker (1981) found they increase message processing mainly when people are not already motivated to think about the message; later work found they work best at the end of an argument (Howard 1990) and can create attitudes that resist counter-argument (Blankenship & Craig 2006) — [Blankenship & Craig 2006](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%204/BlankenshipCraig2006.pdf)
- Sales link is weak: a 2025 PRISMA review of short-form video research reports that likes and comments were weak predictors of sales in one cited study — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399682084_Short-Form_Video_Algorithms_the_Attention_Economy_and_Marketing_Outcomes_A_PRISMA-Based_Systematic_Review)

### Inferences

| Mechanism | Tag | Script example (FR) | Reviewer check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social proof | STRONG EVIDENCE in general (Cialdini tradition; not re-sourced, see Gaps) | "2 300 personnes ont déjà téléchargé le modèle." | The number is real, dated and provable; no "tout le monde en parle" |
| Authority and source credibility | STRONG EVIDENCE in general | "Je suis kiné depuis 12 ans, voici l'exercice que je donne en premier." | The credential is true and relevant to the claim |
| Reciprocity (give value first) | STRONG EVIDENCE in general; untested for Reels | Give the full tip in the video, then offer the template | The video is useful without the CTA |
| Micro-commitment ("commente GUIDE") as foot-in-the-door | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (small effect; unrelated requests weaken it) | "Commente PLAN et je t'envoie le plan en message." | The comment request is directly tied to what the viewer gets; see platform rule in section 8 |
| Scarcity and urgency | STRONG EVIDENCE that it works; legal risk if false | "Les inscriptions ferment dimanche à 20 h." | The deadline or stock limit is real and will be enforced; otherwise it is an unlawful practice (section 8) |
| Loss framing | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS | "Chaque Reel sans sous-titres, c'est des spectateurs qui partent sans le son." | The loss is real and not exaggerated; no invented figure |
| "Parce que" + reason | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (single 1978 field study; placebic reasons reportedly fail for large requests) | "Enregistre cette vidéo, parce que tu en auras besoin au moment de monter." | Every CTA carries a real reason; do not rely on empty reasons |
| Liking, similarity, parasocial bond | STRONG EVIDENCE of association; causality less clear | Speak to camera, use "je" and "tu", show the real workspace | Consistent on-screen person and tone across videos |
| Central versus peripheral route (Elaboration Likelihood Model) | STRONG EVIDENCE as a framework | Low-involvement scroll: one clear cue. Interested viewer: one strong argument with proof | One main argument per video, backed by proof, not five weak ones |
| Fogg model for CTAs (motivation, ability, prompt) | PRACTITIONER LORE (design heuristic, not a tested quantitative model) | One CTA, lowest-effort action first: "Enregistre" before "Inscris-toi" | Exactly one CTA; it can be done in one tap or one word |

- For a viewer scrolling at low involvement, cues (who is speaking, how many others did it, ease of action) matter more than argument detail; argument quality matters once the viewer is interested. That is the practical content of the Elaboration Likelihood Model for scripts.
- Engagement is not purchase: the weak link between likes or comments and sales means persuasion scripts should be judged on the action they target.

### Gaps
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Cialdini's seven principles (reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, unity) come from "Influence" (1984; unity added in "Pre-Suasion", 2016). I found no session source for a meta-analysis of each principle; reciprocity and social proof searches were not run before the budget ended.
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) In Langer's study, I recall that for the small request compliance was about 60% with no reason, 93% with the placebic reason and 94% with the real reason, and that for the large request the placebic reason did no better than no reason (about 24% each versus 42% for a real reason). The PDF linked above should confirm this.
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Many Labs 1 (Klein et al., 2014) replicated gain/loss framing and anchoring across 36 samples. The search for it did not run.
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Petty & Cacioppo's Elaboration Likelihood Model (1986) and BJ Fogg's behaviour model (2009) were not re-sourced. I know of no rigorous validation of the Fogg model as a predictive formula.
- The pooled effect size of the scarcity meta-analysis was not in the abstract.

## 5. Comment-driving mechanisms

### Takeaway
There is almost no peer-reviewed research on what makes people comment on short videos. The usable evidence is indirect: moderate, low-stakes controversy starts conversation, small related requests get more compliance than large ones, and platforms penalise requests whose only purpose is to inflate engagement. Deliberate mistakes ("mistake bait") have no research support and carry credibility and legal risk.

### Cited Findings
- Moderate controversy raises the likelihood of discussion; high controversy lowers it; discomfort rises when identity is visible — [Chen & Berger 2013](https://repository.upenn.edu/handle/20.500.14332/39527)
- In political TikTok videos during the 2025 German federal election, moralised language and high-arousal emotions were the strongest predictors of active engagement (preprint) — [arXiv 2509.10336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.10336)
- Instagram's own guidance separates engagement bait from legitimate prompts: it is bait "if the end goal is to simply maximize the quantity of engagements as a way to 'hack' reach rather than connect with their audience", whereas "earnestly engaging with your audience in the comments and DMs to connect personally, generate leads, or drive conversions is not considered to be engagement bait" — [Social Media Today on Instagram's clarification](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-advice-on-single-word-ctas-and-longer-reels/718151/)
- Instagram's Recommendations Guidelines list engagement bait alongside clickbait as content it avoids recommending; repeat offenders may lose recommendation eligibility for a period — [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/313829416281232)
- A 2025 IAE Paris research note on brands' directive language on social networks concludes that overly directive language can embarrass consumers and curb their engagement — [IAE Paris](https://www.iae-paris.com/sites/default/files/2025-10/2025_10_MVS_CPR_%20Le%20langage%20directif_0.pdf)
- Foot-in-the-door effects are small and weaken when the first and second requests are unrelated — [Burger 1999 review](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/burger/2497673)

### Inferences

| Mechanism | Tag | Script example (FR) | Reviewer check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-effort question tied to the content | PRACTITIONER LORE (consistent with ease-of-action logic) | "Tu montes sur CapCut ou sur Premiere ? Un mot en commentaire." | Answerable in one to three words; the creator would actually read the answers |
| Either/or choice on a low-stakes topic | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (extrapolated from Chen & Berger) | "Hook parlé ou hook écrit : tu choisis lequel ?" | Both options are defensible; topic is taste or method, not identity |
| Hot take that invites disagreement | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS | "Les hashtags ne servent plus à rien. Change-moi d'avis." | The creator holds and argues the view; it is not a strawman built for outrage |
| Keyword comment for a resource | PRACTITIONER LORE; allowed by Instagram when it serves real lead generation | "Commente MODÈLE, je te l'envoie." | The resource exists and is delivered to everyone who asks |
| Niche in-group reference | PRACTITIONER LORE | "Si tu as connu l'algorithme de 2019, tu sais." | The target audience gets it without explanation |
| Deliberate mistake or omission | PRACTITIONER LORE; ethically weak | — | Reject. If the "mistake" concerns a fact, price or product feature, it becomes a misleading claim |
| "Commente OUI si tu es d'accord", "tague 3 amis" | PRACTITIONER LORE; penalised | — | Reject: this is the pattern platforms define as engagement bait |

- A simple test for reviewers: would the question still be worth asking if comments did not affect reach? If yes, it is a conversation; if no, it is bait.
- The French finding on directive language suggests softening imperatives in CTAs ("dis-moi", "tu me diras") rather than stacking orders ("Like, commente, partage").

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found on comment prompts, either/or questions, or deliberate errors in short-form video. Everything in the table beyond controversy and platform rules is inference or practitioner opinion.
- The IAE Paris note was seen only through a search summary; its method and sample are unknown.
- Meta's Transparency Center page on engagement bait (the official definition and categories) could not be opened (host blocked). The five-category list often quoted (vote, react, share, tag, comment bait) dates from Meta's 2017–2018 announcements and is unverified here.

## 6. Language level: which word-level findings hold up

### Takeaway
Second-person address, concrete and sensory words, simple wording and well-placed rhetorical questions have peer-reviewed support, mostly from English-language text. "Power word" lists have no research behind them, precise numbers help credibility only in some contexts, and evidence specific to French copy is thin (one exploratory paper on tutoiement, one note on directive language).

### Cited Findings
- "You": second-person pronouns in brand posts increase involvement and brand attitude via self-referencing — [Cruz, Leonhardt & Pezzuti 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v39y2017icp104-116.html)
- Concreteness: concrete words raise satisfaction and purchases in real service interactions — [Packard & Berger 2021, via Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/how-language-boosts-customer-satisfaction/)
- Sensory words raise likes, comments and purchase intent for influencer posts on Instagram and TikTok — [Cascio Rizzo et al. 2023](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/Sesory-Language-and-Influencers.pdf)
- Certainty words ("always", "everything", "forever") in brand posts on Facebook and Twitter are associated with more likes, comments and shares, because the brand seems more powerful; the effect is stronger for audiences with high power-distance beliefs — [Pezzuti, Leonhardt & Warren 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v53y2021icp32-46.html)
- But certainty-laden emotion reduces sustained attention, while uncertainty holds it — [Berger, Moe & Schweidel 2023, AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2023/03/21/the-challenge-of-keeping-an-audience-engaged-how-language-shapes-attention/)
- Simple, common words and shorter headlines get more clicks; the gain per headline is small — [Shulman, Markowitz & Rogers 2024, Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2024-06-simple-headlines-online-news-readers.html)
- Negative words raise click-through, positive words lower it, on about 105,000 headline variants — [Robertson et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)
- Precise numbers: Schindler & Yalch (2006) report that sharp (non-round) numbers in ad claims are perceived as more factual — [Boston College research brief](https://bc.edu/content/ccc/research-briefs/do-decimals-add-credibility-.html). The effect reverses in some settings: Wadhwa & Zhang found round numbers worked better in health messages — [Wadhwa & Zhang](https://wiki.agepoly.ch/finance/sponsoring/wadhwa-zhang-2015.pdf)
- Rhetorical questions increase thinking about the message mainly among viewers who were not already engaged — [Blankenship & Craig 2006](https://www.uky.edu/~ngrant/CJT780/readings/Day%204/BlankenshipCraig2006.pdf)
- French: an exploratory paper by Catherine Aussilhou (Université Côte d'Azur) on tutoiement versus vouvoiement in brand posts reports positive effects of "tu" on engagement, notes possible contradictory effects, and states that no prior study had examined tutoiement norms on social networks — [conference paper](https://events.unamur.be/event/81/attachments/97/218/papier%206-3.pdf)
- French: advertising's growing use of "tu" is observed in the press (IKEA as an early example); this is commentary, not measurement — [Le Temps](https://www.letemps.ch/opinions/partout-declin-vouvoiement)

### Inferences

| Word-level rule | Tag | Script example (FR) | Reviewer check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Address the viewer as "tu" / "vous" | STRONG EVIDENCE for second person (English data); WEAK evidence for tu versus vous | "Tu perds la moitié de tes spectateurs ici." | "Tu" or "vous" appears in the first sentence; the choice is consistent across the script and fits the brand |
| Concrete over abstract | STRONG EVIDENCE | "Mets ton sous-titre à 1/3 du haut de l'écran" not "optimise ta mise en page" | Each instruction can be filmed or pictured |
| Sensory words for products | STRONG EVIDENCE | "Une mousse dense, qui tient sur la cuillère" | At least one sensory descriptor per product mention |
| Short sentences, common words | STRONG EVIDENCE (small effect) | "Regarde. Trois secondes. C'est tout ce que tu as." | Readable aloud in one breath; no subordinate clause in the hook |
| Confident wording when giving the answer | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (brand posts; trades off against attention and against honesty) | "Ça marche à chaque fois que le hook tient en 6 mots" only if true | No "toujours", "jamais", "garanti" on a claim that cannot be proven |
| Precise numbers | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS | "41 % de rétention à 3 secondes" | The number is real; precision is not fabricated |
| Odd numbers in lists ("7 astuces") | PRACTITIONER LORE | — | No rule; use the true count |
| Rhetorical question | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (conditional effect) | "Tu sais pourquoi ton Reel s'arrête à 200 vues ?" | At most one in the hook; the video answers it |
| "Power words" lists ("secret", "incroyable", "choc") | PRACTITIONER LORE | — | Do not score scripts on presence of list words; flag empty superlatives |
| Negations | No evidence found either way for scripts | — | Prefer the affirmative form for instructions, as a clarity choice, not a proven effect |
| Active voice | PRACTITIONER LORE supported indirectly by fluency findings | "Coupe le silence" not "le silence doit être coupé" | Instructions use an active verb |

- The NLP claim that "the brain cannot process negation" is not something I found any support for. Preferring affirmative instructions is defensible only as a clarity habit.
- Tutoiement is a brand-voice decision in French, with one exploratory study suggesting a positive effect on engagement. It should be set once per brand, not optimised per video.

### Gaps
- (Background knowledge, not verified this session) Packard, Berger & Boghrati's work on verb tense (present tense makes reviews more persuasive) and Packard & Berger's "Thinking of You" (second-person pronouns in songs) exist in my memory but were not re-sourced.
- No peer-reviewed study of French-language short-video scripts was found. Almost all word-level evidence is from English text; transfer to spoken French is assumed.
- No study testing "power word" lists, odd-number lists or active versus passive voice on social video was found.
- The Aussilhou paper's sample and effect sizes were not visible in the search summary.

## 7. What research on short-form video and TikTok specifically shows (2021–2026)

### Takeaway
The academic literature specific to short video is young, mostly correlational and often small-sample or unreviewed. Consistent signals: creator audience size dominates, on-screen text and close framing help, emotion matters modestly (surprise, fear, high arousal, moral language), and the algorithm itself confounds every content-to-engagement correlation. No rigorous study was found for trending audio, pace or cut rate.

### Cited Findings
- Ling et al., "Slapping Cats, Bopping Heads, and Oreo Shakes" (ACM Web Science 2022): 400 labelled TikTok videos, likes as the virality measure. The creator's follower count is the most powerful predictor; close-up or medium-scale shots and second-person point of view help; unlike image memes, including text in the video helps virality — [NSF public access copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10399977); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.02452)
- Zannettou et al. (CHI 2024): data donations from 347 TikTok users covering 9.2 million recommended videos, analysing how users engage with recommendations — [University of Washington PDF](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~franzi/pdf/zannettou-tiktok-chi24.pdf)
- University of Chicago thesis (2026): emotional cues (audio, facial expression, timing) have low but significant explanatory power for shares and comments; surprise and fear are consistently positive; algorithmic promotion partly drives the links — [UChicago Knowledge](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/rs03s-c0f50)
- University of Twente MSc thesis (eye tracking, TikTok ads): a three-second engagement threshold; text elements are the first fixation point more often than faces; native-looking visuals hold viewers longer than high-production ones — [University of Twente](https://essay.utwente.nl/fileshare/file/109005/Daphne_Giuzio_MSc_Thesis.pdf)
- 2025 German federal election study (preprint): moralised language and high-arousal emotion best predict active engagement on political TikTok — [arXiv 2509.10336](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.10336)
- Finfluencer study (preprint): video duration alone was not meaningfully associated with engagement — [arXiv 2607.05203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.05203)
- Method warning: a paper in PMC argues TikTok content analyses must account for follower count when interpreting engagement — [PMC10865194](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10865194/)
- A 2025 PRISMA review of short-form video research reports humour and emotion in brand content are linked to more likes, comments and shares, and that likes and comments weakly predict sales — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399682084_Short-Form_Video_Algorithms_the_Attention_Economy_and_Marketing_Outcomes_A_PRISMA-Based_Systematic_Review)

### Inferences

| Feature | Tag | Script-level use (FR) | Reviewer check |
|---|---|---|---|
| On-screen text hook in frame one | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (one small classifier study, one thesis; consistent direction) | Text: "3 erreurs qui coulent ton Reel" over the first shot | The script specifies the exact on-screen text for the first 2 seconds, 8 words or fewer |
| Close or medium shot, speaking to the viewer | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS | Face-camera, "tu" | Shot type is specified for the hook |
| Surprise in the opening | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS | "J'ai supprimé mon meilleur Reel. Exprès." | The opening contains an unexpected element relevant to the topic |
| Native look over polished production | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (one thesis, ads) | Shot on phone, real setting | No requirement for studio polish |
| Humour and emotion | MIXED OR REPLICATION CONCERNS (review-level, correlational) | One light beat before the CTA | The script has a named emotional beat |
| Trending audio | PRACTITIONER LORE (no study found) | — | Not a scoring criterion |
| Fast pace, cut every N seconds | PRACTITIONER LORE (no study found) | — | Not a scoring criterion beyond "no dead air in the first 3 seconds" |
| Optimal length | No reliable evidence (one preprint finds duration unrelated to engagement) | — | Length follows content |

- Follower count dominating virality means that comparing a script's performance across accounts says little about the script. Evaluate against the same account's baseline.
- Since the recommender amplifies what already gets engagement, observational "feature X predicts likes" studies overstate what a writer can cause by adding feature X.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study in a top marketing journal specifically on Reels or TikTok script features was found before the search budget ran out; a targeted query for 2022–2025 content analyses did not execute.
- The Ling et al. effect sizes and the truncated follower-count sentence were not verified against the full text (arxiv.org blocked).
- Two of the sources are student theses and two are preprints; none should be quoted as settled findings.
- No academic evidence found on hooks' "first 3 seconds" retention thresholds beyond the Twente thesis; platform-published retention guidance belongs to the adjacent research track on algorithms.

## 8. The ethical and legal line (EU and France, platform rules)

### Takeaway
Persuasion techniques become unlawful in France and the EU when they rest on something false or hidden: fake urgency, invented numbers or reviews, or undisclosed commercial intent. French law puts deceptive practices at up to two years' imprisonment and a €300,000 fine, the 2023 influencer law (amended by ordinance in November 2024) requires commercial intent to be disclosed, and Instagram withholds recommendation from engagement bait.

### Cited Findings
- Code de la consommation, article L121-4 lists practices deemed misleading in all circumstances, including falsely stating that a product or service will only be available for a very limited time in order to obtain an immediate decision — [Légifrance, article L121-4](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/article_lc/LEGIARTI000044563107). A law-firm summary describes the list as covering false "free" offers, false purchase urgency and biased reviews — [HLC Avocats](https://hlc.com/fr/publications/misleading-commercial-practices-a-stricter-legal-framework-increased-controls-and-best-practices)
- Article L132-2 punishes the deceptive practices defined in L121-2 to L121-4 with two years' imprisonment and a €300,000 fine — [French Business Law, article L132-2](https://french-business-law.com/rench-legislation-art/article-l132-2-of-the-french-consumer-code)
- Loi n° 2023-451 of 9 June 2023 (published 10 June 2023) created a legal definition of the commercial influencer and originally required the label "Publicité" or "Collaboration commerciale" on promotional content; breaches can bring imprisonment, fines of up to €300,000 in some cases and a ban on practising — [Vie publique](https://www.vie-publique.fr/loi/288793-loi-influenceurs-proposition-de-loi-delaporte-vojetta); [Actu-Juridique](https://www.actu-juridique.fr/breves/affaires/reseaux-sociaux-loi-visant-a-encadrer-linfluence-commerciale-et-a-lutter-contre-les-derives-des-influenceurs/)
- Ordonnance n° 2024-978 of 6 November 2024 (in force 8 November 2024) amended the law to comply with EU law: a new article 5-2 allows commercial intent to be shown by "Publicité", "Collaboration commerciale" or an equivalent mention suited to the format, and failure to indicate it counts as a misleading practice only where the commercial intent is not already apparent from the context — [Vie publique](https://www.vie-publique.fr/loi/296047-ordonnance-du-6-novembre-2024-modifiant-la-loi-influenceurs-de-2023); [Lettre des Réseaux](https://www.lettredesreseaux.com/influence-commerciale-assouplissement-des-obligations-dinformation-reposant-sur-linfluenceur.html); [Kolsquare](https://www.kolsquare.com/fr/blog/loi-influenceurs-modifiee-suite-aux-preoccupations-de-la-commission-europeenne-et-de-lindustrie)
- A décret n° 2026-233 of 30 March 2026 on the mandatory mentions in each promotional action is reported; its content was not consulted — [Le Monde du Droit](https://www.lemondedudroit.fr/affaires/295-consommation/103889-influenceurs-mentions-obligatoires-devant-figurer-dans-le-contenu-de-chaque-action-promotionnelle.html)
- Enforcement: the DGCCRF sanctioned around twenty influencers in the months after the law for undisclosed sponsorship or prohibited promotions, and since September 2023 can attach daily penalties to its injunctions (total capped at €50,000) — [Dreyfus](https://dreyfus.fr/en/2024/10/01/review-of-the-effectiveness-of-the-french-law-n-2023-451-of-9-june-2023-regulating-influencers-after-its-first-year-of-implementation); [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2023/france/dgccrf-new-power-to-impose-daily-penalties-on-its-injunctions-for-breach-of-the-french-consumer-code)

### Inferences

Reviewer's red-line checklist (any "yes" fails the script):
1. Does the script state or presuppose a fact, number, result or testimonial that cannot be proven? (Misleading practice; includes "9 personnes sur 10", fake before/after, invented client results.)
2. Does it announce a deadline, limited stock or "dernières places" that is not real or will be quietly extended? (Deemed misleading in all circumstances under L121-4.)
3. Is there a commercial relationship (payment, gift, affiliate link, own product) that a viewer would not clearly understand from the video itself? If so, is "Publicité", "Collaboration commerciale" or an equivalent clear mention present for the whole promotion?
4. Does the hook promise something the video does not deliver? (Clickbait: platform demotion, and misleading if it concerns an offer.)
5. Does it ask for likes, comments, tags or shares with no purpose other than reach ("commente OUI", "tague 3 amis pour…")?
6. Does it use fear, shame or a vulnerability (health, money trouble, body image) to push a purchase?
7. Does it contain a deliberate error or omission designed to provoke corrections?

- The line between persuasion and manipulation that fits both the law and the evidence: a technique is acceptable if it would still work once the viewer understood exactly what was being done and why. Real scarcity, real social proof and an openly stated CTA pass; presupposed false facts and hidden commercial intent do not.
- The most effective evidence-backed levers (concreteness, specificity, proof, real numbers) are also the ones that keep a script lawful, because they force claims to be checkable.
